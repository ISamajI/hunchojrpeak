Food Evidence Guide

What the research says about foods that help you lose weight, foods that keep you healthy, and foods worth cutting back on.

Weight loss comes from eating fewer calories than you burn.

No food burns fat on its own. The foods that help are the ones that fill you up for few calories, so the deficit is easier to keep.

Evidence: Strong Moderate Weak Not yet checked

Foods for losing weight

Why some foods help

Protein

Moderate

Keeps you fuller and helps you keep muscle while you lose fat. A meta-analysis of 24 trials found slightly more fat lost and about 0.4 kg less muscle lost.

Bulky, watery foods

Strong

Vegetables, broth soups and fruit fill you up for few calories. In a year-long trial, people who ate soup twice a day lost about 50% more weight than people eating snack foods with the same calories.

Fiber

Weak

People who eat more fiber gain less weight over time, but trials of fiber foods alone mostly show no weight loss. It helps by making meals bulkier, not on its own.

Less ultra-processed food

Moderate

A 2025 trial found people lost about 2% of body weight in 8 weeks on a minimally processed diet, versus 1% on an ultra-processed one. It was one small study.

Eat more of these
  • Beans, lentils, chickpeasProtein and fiber. About 1 serving a day added 0.34 kg extra loss in trials.
  • EggsLots of protein for the calories; filling at breakfast.
  • Plain Greek yogurt, cottage cheeseProtein-dense and low in calories when unsweetened.
  • Chicken and fishHigh protein for few calories.
  • Tofu, tempeh, edamamePlant protein plus fiber.
  • Leafy greens, broccoli, cauliflowerVery few calories for a lot of food.
  • Berries, apples, pearsFiber and water; linked to less weight gain.
  • OatsThick fiber that keeps you full longer than refined cereal.
  • Boiled or baked potatoesTop of 38 foods for fullness in an older study. Fries don't count.
  • Broth-based soupsBefore a meal, cut how much people ate by about 20%.
  • Nuts, about 1 oz a dayTrials show no weight gain at this amount.

Beans, nuts, soups and potatoes have studies behind them. Eggs, yogurt, oats, chicken, fish and tofu are based on the protein and fiber evidence above Not yet checked

Easy swaps
  • Soda or juice→Water or whole fruit
  • Pastries or sugary cereal→Oats with berries and yogurt
  • Fries or chips→Baked or boiled potato
  • Crackers or crisps→A handful of nuts, or veggies with hummus
  • Sweetened yogurt→Plain Greek yogurt with fruit
  • Straight to the main meal→Broth soup or big salad first
Weight-loss myths
  • Celery, grapefruit and apple cider vinegar don't burn fat.
  • Nuts don't make you fat at normal portions.
  • Carbs aren't automatically fattening. How potatoes are cooked matters more than the potato.
  • High-fiber foods alone won't cause weight loss without eating fewer calories overall.

Diet patterns
  • Mediterranean has the best evidence, including a randomized trial showing fewer heart attacks and strokes.
  • DASH lowers blood pressure.
  • MIND is aimed at the brain. Early studies looked good, but a trial found no clear benefit.
  • The exact diet matters less than the shared basics: mostly plants, whole grains, beans, nuts, fish and healthy oils.
Overhyped

Goji, acai, spirulina and turmeric are fine to eat but no better than ordinary fruit and vegetables. Coconut oil raises LDL ("bad") cholesterol. Not yet checked

Wrongly blamed

These foods get a worse reputation than the evidence supports. Not yet checked

  • EggsAbout 1 a day shows no clear heart risk for most people. The signal is stronger in people with diabetes.
  • PotatoesPlain boiled or baked are fine. The risk is mostly from fries and chips.
  • Whole-fat dairyMostly neutral. Yogurt may be slightly helpful.
  • Whole fruitNatural fruit sugar is fine and fruit lowers disease risk. Keep juice small.
  • Seed oilsSwapping butter for them lowers LDL. The real concern is frying oil that's reused.

Before you rely on this

  • Most diet research follows people's habits over time, so it shows links, not proof of cause.
  • Items marked Not yet checked came from background knowledge, not a source checked during this research.
  • This is general information, not medical advice. If you have a health condition, talk to a doctor or dietitian.
